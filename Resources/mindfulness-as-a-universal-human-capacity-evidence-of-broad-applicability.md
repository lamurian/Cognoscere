## Summary

The hypothesis that mindfulness is broadly applicable across populations is supported by converging evidence, but requires important qualifications that are frequently overlooked in popular presentations.

Mindfulness, defined as "paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally" (Kabat-Zinn, 1994), is fundamentally a cognitive capacity for metacognitive awareness that requires no special resources, equipment, or financial investment to *attempt*. As a conceptual/psychological proposition, the claim that humans possess the capacity for present-moment awareness is broadly plausible and defensible.

Mitsea et al. [@mitsea2023] explicitly note: "Although everyone has the potential to practice mindfulness, mindfulness training interventions can be highly effortful and challenging, especially for novices." This acknowledges both the universality of the underlying capacity and the practical barriers to sustained, effective practice.

**Evidence for cross-population effectiveness (Proposition B):**

Hytman et al. [@hytman2025] conducted a systematic narrative review of culturally adapted mindfulness-based interventions among ethno-racial minority populations. Only 10 studies met their inclusion criteria — an encouraging but small evidence base. This demonstrates that mindfulness interventions *can be* adapted for culturally diverse populations, but does not establish equivalent effectiveness across all populations. Study quality, heterogeneity, and underrepresentation remain significant problems.

**The digital access caveat:**

Digital delivery mechanisms — including AI chatbots, VR/AR, and mobile phone-based interventions — have shown promise in reducing some barriers to mindfulness access (Mitsea et al., [@mitsea2023]). However, digital access itself is socioeconomically patterned: populations facing poverty, homelessness, or unstable housing may have limited or unreliable access to smartphones, internet connectivity, or quiet spaces for practice. Therefore, digital technology should be treated as a *potential* access-enabler, not as evidence that socioeconomic barriers to mindfulness have disappeared.

**The critical distinction:**

The statement "everyone has the potential to practice mindfulness" is reasonable as a conceptual proposition about human capacity. It is *not* equivalent to "scientific evidence proves mindfulness is equally effective for everyone." The latter is not established. Evidence supports broad applicability with important qualifications about heterogeneous effects, variable study quality, and context-dependent outcomes.

**Propositions A, B, and C:**

- **A. Universality** (Can humans generally develop/use mindfulness?): Broadly plausible and supported.
- **B. Cross-population effectiveness** (Do interventions work across all populations?): Supported with important qualifications — heterogeneity, small evidence bases for some groups, and underrepresentation remain problems.
- **C. Foundationality** (Is mindfulness a fundamental causal building block of resilience?): Not established by the evidence reviewed here.
