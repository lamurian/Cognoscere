## Summary

Preschoolers aged 3 to 5 represent a transitional period in screen time guidelines. Both the AAP and WHO recommend limiting recreational screen time to approximately one hour per day of high-quality programming. The content matters significantly at this age: educational media designed around learning goals in mathematics and reading can be developmentally appropriate, while passive entertainment viewing carries greater risk.

Research on executive function reveals that excessive screen time during the preschool years is associated with measurable cognitive deficits. Fitzpatrick et al. @fitzpatrick2025 found that preschoolers with high screen use scored lower on three key executive function domains: inhibitory control (suppressing impulsive behaviors), cognitive flexibility (creative problem-solving), and working memory (managing thoughts and emotions). These skills form the foundation for academic readiness and self-regulation.

Short-term impacts include disrupted attention spans, difficulty with emotional regulation, and reduced engagement in imaginative play. Long-term concerns center on school readiness and academic trajectories. Children who spend excessive time on screens during preschool years may enter kindergarten with weaker foundational skills. However, when screen use is limited, co-viewed with caregivers, and focused on high-quality educational content, the negative effects can be substantially mitigated. The AAP's updated framework emphasizes context over raw hours, encouraging families to consider what activities screens displace.

## Key Points

- Recommended maximum: 1 hour/day of high-quality programming
- High-quality content: PBS Kids, Sesame Workshop, educational apps aligned with learning goals
- Short-term risks: reduced attention, impaired emotional regulation, less imaginative play
- Long-term risks: weaker executive function, reduced school readiness, delayed academic skills
- Executive function domains affected: inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, working memory
- Co-viewing with caregivers and discussing content enhances educational value
