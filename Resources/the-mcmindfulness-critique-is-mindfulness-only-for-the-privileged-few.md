## Summary

The "McMindfulness" critique, coined by Purser and Loy in a viral 2013 Huffington Post post and extended in Purser's 2019 book *McMindfulness*, argues that mindfulness has been commodified, instrumentalized, and depoliticized by corporate and wellness industries. The critique holds that secular mindfulness has become part of a "Mindfulness Industrial Complex" (Ferguson, 2016) that serves neoliberal agendas rather than genuine human well-being.

Leggett [@leggett2021] provides a thorough academic analysis of this critique, confirming several of its key claims. Mindfulness has been widely institutionalized in workplaces, schools, public health, criminal rehabilitation, and even the military — often as a tool for enhancing productivity and self-optimization rather than addressing structural inequalities. The critique argues that mindfulness functions as a "technology of the self" that constructs neoliberal subjects who blame themselves rather than systems for their distress.

A central concern is that mindfulness has been de-politicized: by focusing on individual awareness and present-moment attention, it can deflect from the social and structural roots of suffering. As Leggett [@leggett2021] reports, critics note that mindfulness can lead practitioners to think "I need to fix myself before I fix the world," permanently deferring political engagement. The "pure awareness" promoted in some mindfulness traditions is argued to be ontologically impossible and to negate critical capacities.

However, the McMindfulness critique has been challenged. Anālayo [@anlayo2019] provides a peer-reviewed critique of the McMindfulness thesis, arguing that the indiscriminate application of the term is problematic. Anālayo acknowledges genuine commercialization and problematic uses of mindfulness, including military applications, but argues that these do not establish that mindfulness-based healthcare itself is inherently an instrument of neoliberal control. The constructionist approach to meditation practice — which emphasizes cognitive engagement, ethical evaluation, and social context — offers a counter-narrative to purely instrumentalized mindfulness.

**The strongest version of the critique:**
Some critics make a substantive argument that particular forms of mindfulness practice can individualize suffering by directing attention toward personal regulation rather than structural causes. This is a legitimate philosophical and sociological argument, even if it does not demonstrate that mindfulness is ineffective. It is distinct from the claim that mindfulness is only for the privileged.

**Key distinctions:**

> Criticism of the commercialization and institutional co-optation of mindfulness ≠ evidence against mindfulness as a psychological practice.

The McMindfulness critique identifies genuine problems with how mindfulness has been deployed in corporate, military, and political contexts. But these problems concern delivery, institutional context, and political economy — not the validity of mindfulness as a psychological capacity or intervention.
