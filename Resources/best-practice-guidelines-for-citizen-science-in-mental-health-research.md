## Summary

Citizen science enables partnering with the public at scale in research, but guidance specific to mental health was scarce until Todowede et al. (2023) published the first systematic review and evidence synthesis in this area, conducted as part of the C-STACS study [@todowede2023]. Screening 14,063 documents, the review found only nine eligible studies, all from high-income countries, signalling that mental health citizen science is feasible but nascent. Most citizen scientists participated as contributors during data collection; reporting against the ten ECSA principles was often unclear or unstated [@todowede2023].

The review produced a ten-point best practice guideline mapping each ECSA principle to mental health-specific considerations and reporting recommendations. Key themes: involving people with lived experience from project inception through leadership, safeguarding against harms from peer-to-peer interaction (including health-related advice shared between contributors), early co-creation of data management plans, explicit reporting of consent processes and data ownership, and evaluating benefits for contributors as well as researchers [@todowede2023].

The guidelines also recommend using existing health research reporting standards (GRIPP2, PICO/SPICE/SPIDER), stating participation level at each research stage, naming the citizen science platform and data repository, and acknowledging citizen scientists as a group and individually with permission. Citizen science is positioned as complementary to, not a replacement for, established participatory approaches in mental health such as co-design and co-production [@todowede2023].

## Key Points

- **Involvement**: Citizen scientists should include people with lived experience (service users, carers, clinicians, public) from study development to feedback, in roles from contributor to project leader. State level and type of involvement explicitly [@todowede2023].
- **Genuine science outcome**: Apply citizen science to knowledge gaps suited to co-developed, person-centred, community-focused designs; involve policymakers and stakeholders in co-design to ensure research leads to change [@todowede2023].
- **Benefits**: Identify planned benefits for contributors and researchers from the outset; evaluate contributor views on benefits; reimburse more active roles (e.g., project leaders), though participation payment is not the norm [@todowede2023].
- **Stages of participation**: Encourage participation at all research stages — co-design, data collection, analysis, dissemination; peer researchers and leadership boards enable this [@todowede2023].
- **Feedback**: Build a co-developed feedback approach from the start informing citizen scientists about key findings and their use; most reviewed studies reported no feedback mechanism [@todowede2023].
- **Safeguarding**: Protect people with lived experience from harms arising from contributor-to-contributor interaction, unsolicited health advice, and mismatched expectations; moderation is essential [@todowede2023].
- **Data availability**: Open data is consistent with citizen science principles but rarely implemented due to privacy concerns; co-create a data management plan early and follow GDPR and local information governance [@todowede2023].
- **Acknowledgement**: Thank citizen scientists as an unnamed group in all presentations; name individuals with permission; agree wording early; involve contributors in co-authoring [@todowede2023].
- **Evaluation**: Cover scientific outputs, data quality, participant experience, researcher experience, and societal/policy impact [@todowede2023].
- **Legal and ethical**: Project-specific consent only needed where personal information is collected; address technology security, anonymisation, safeguarding, and IP collection explicitly [@todowede2023].
