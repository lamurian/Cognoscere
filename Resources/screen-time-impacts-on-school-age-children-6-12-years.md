## Summary

School-age children aged 6 to 12 occupy a complex middle ground in the screen time debate. The AAP retired its previous 2-hour daily limit in January 2026, replacing fixed hourly caps with a holistic framework that considers content quality, context, and the child's individual characteristics. This shift recognizes that screens now permeate education, socialization, and daily life in ways that make rigid time limits impractical and potentially counterproductive.

Physical health impacts become more measurable in this age group. Horner et al. @horner2025 found that every additional hour of recreational screen time was associated with an increase of approximately 0.08 standard deviations in cardiometabolic risk scores among 10-year-olds. This risk compounds over time: a child with three extra hours of daily screen time would have roughly a quarter to half a standard-deviation higher cardiometabolic risk than peers. Sleep emerged as a critical moderating factor, with about 12% of the association between screen time and cardiometabolic risk mediated through shorter sleep duration.

Cognitively, excessive screen time is linked to reduced academic performance and attention difficulties. An NIH study found that children with more than seven hours of daily screen time experienced thinning of the brain's cortex, the region responsible for critical thinking and reasoning. Social-emotional risks include exposure to cyberbullying, online safety concerns, and displacement of physical activity. The AAP now recommends families develop a 'media plan' using their Family Media Plan tool, distinguishing between constructive uses (video-chatting with friends, educational apps, creative projects) and passive consumption (scrolling algorithmic feeds).

## Key Points

- Recommended maximum: no fixed limit; context-dependent per AAP 2026 guidance
- Previous recommendation (retired): 2 hours/day of recreational screen time
- Cardiometabolic risk increases ~0.08 SD per additional hour of screen time
- Cortical thinning observed with >7 hours/day in NIH research
- Sleep disruption is a key mediating factor (12% of cardiometabolic association)
- Distinguish between active/creative use vs. passive consumption
- AAP Family Media Plan recommended for household screen boundaries
