## Summary

The China-Russia relationship has deepened into a quasi-alliance that represents the most significant bilateral strategic partnership challenging U.S. global primacy since the Cold War. The CFR Special Report characterizes it as posing "the greatest threat to vital U.S. national interests in sixty years" (@goldberg2024). Xi Jinping told Putin in March 2023: "Right now there are changes — the likes of which we haven't seen for 100 years — and we are the ones driving these changes together."

The partnership pursues six core strategic objectives: replacing the U.S. as the primary global actor; eroding U.S. influence across Europe, the Indo-Pacific, the Middle East, and the Global South; weakening the U.S. alliance system and extended nuclear deterrence; shifting regional military balances; undermining confidence in U.S. credibility; and preventing democratic values from threatening authoritarian domestic power. The May 2024 joint statement explicitly committed to countering "Washington's destructive and hostile course towards the so-called 'dual containment'" (@goldberg2024).

Skeptics point to asymmetries: Russia's stagnating economy versus China's continued growth; lingering border disputes; competition in Central Asia and the Arctic; and China's discomfort with Russian nuclear saber-rutting. However, the CFR report argues these tactical differences are dwarfed by strategic convergence. Economic integration has deepened — Russia has become China's primary goods supplier replacing European sources, while China secured reliable Siberian hydrocarbon flows. Pentagon reports confirm increasing Arctic collaboration across multiple instruments of national power.

The relationship is functionally an alliance without formal treaty obligations. Putin declared the two "are allies in every sense of the word." The partnership's durability rests on shared revisionist goals: resisting U.S.-led international order, redefining sovereignty norms against human rights interference, and establishing spheres of influence free from external constraint.

## Key Points

- CFR labels China-Russia quasi-alliance as greatest U.S. threat in 60 years
- Six core objectives: replace U.S. primacy, erode alliances, shift military balances, undermine credibility
- Asymmetries (economic, territorial) exist but are outweighed by strategic convergence
- Economic integration deepened post-2022: Russia as supplier, China as market for hydrocarbons
- Functionally an alliance — shared revisionist goals without formal treaty
