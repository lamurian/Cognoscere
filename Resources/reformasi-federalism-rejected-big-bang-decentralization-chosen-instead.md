## Summary

When Suharto fell in 1998, Indonesia faced its most serious moment for territorial reform, but the answer was not federalism. Ryaas Rasyid, a US-educated professor, convened the "Team of Seven" political scientists who drafted the sweeping autonomy legislation of 1999. Their design deliberately devolved power to the roughly 300 regencies (kabupaten) and municipalities rather than to provinces — on the explicit logic that many Indonesians still associated provincial authority with the federalism of Dutch colonialism, and that 28 strong provinces could become bases for secession, while hundreds of small districts could not coordinate it [@bennet2010].

The reform — Laws 22/1999 and 25/1999 on regional government and fiscal balance — transferred about 2.4 million civil servants and over 40% of government expenditure to local government, one of the largest and fastest decentralisations ever attempted. It was later refined by Laws 32/2004 and 33/2004, which introduced direct elections for mayors, regents, and governors [@bennet2010; @bertrand2019]. Meanwhile, the constitutional amendments (1999–2002) created a weak regional chamber, the DPD, rather than a genuine federal-style upper house: Bertrand notes it is "an anomalous chamber" with no legislative power, created precisely because the political elite feared giving provinces real power [@bertrand2019].

The choice reflected elite consensus: PDI-P and Golkar, plus reformers, all insisted on preserving the unitary state. The result has been mixed — some 190-plus new autonomous entities, "decentralised corruption," and uneven capacity — but also successes such as Joko Widodo's celebrated reform of Surakarta after winning the first direct mayoral election [@bennet2010]. Federalism itself, however, was never seriously on the table: the state chose to diffuse power downward to districts instead of sharing sovereignty territorially [@bennet2010; @tobing2015].

## Key Points

- Rasyid's 1999 laws devolved power to regencies, not provinces, to prevent a federal-style territorial base for separatism.
- 2.4 million civil servants and 40%+ of expenditure moved to local government.
- The DPD (created 2001–2002) is a weak regional chamber — a deliberate refusal of federal-style bicameralism.
- Decentralisation bred "decentralised corruption" and district proliferation but also local success stories like Solo.
