## Summary

The concern that LGBT legal inclusion and acceptance contribute to declining birth rates conflates correlation with causation. The available quantitative evidence suggests both phenomena are driven by the same underlying structural factors -- urbanization, women's education and labor force participation, secularization, and the broader second demographic transition -- rather than one causing the other.

**What the evidence shows:**

A study using OECD country data from 1980-2009 found that legal recognition of same-sex couples had no negative effect on different-sex family formation [@trandafir2015]. The author concludes that concerns about same-sex marriage undermining heterosexual marriage and fertility are not supported by empirical data.

Research on the Netherlands, the first country to legalize same-sex marriage (2001), found that same-sex marriage legalization did not negatively impact different-sex marriage rates [@trandafir2013]. The study used difference-in-difference methods to isolate the causal effect of the law change.

Sweden, which has had registered partnership since 1995 and full same-sex marriage since 2009, provides longitudinal demographic data [@kolk2020]. Swedish fertility rates, while below replacement, follow trends similar to other Nordic countries. Same-sex couples in Sweden have increasing rates of childbearing through assisted reproduction and adoption, though they remain a small fraction of total births.

The **second demographic transition theory** (Lesthaeghe, Van de Kaa) explains declining fertility across developed countries as driven by: rising individualism, gender equality, women's labor force participation, secularization, effective contraception, delayed marriage and childbearing, and higher education costs. These same structural factors are also associated with greater tolerance of LGBT people. The correlation between LGBT acceptance and low birth rates is thus a case of shared causal drivers, not a causal relationship.

**Cross-national pattern:** Countries with highest LGBT acceptance (Nordic countries, Netherlands, Canada) have total fertility rates (TFR) around 1.5-1.8. But countries with lower acceptance also have very low fertility -- Poland (1.3), Japan (1.3), South Korea (0.7). Japan and South Korea have extremely low fertility yet relatively low LGBT acceptance, demonstrating that low birth rates occur independently of LGBT rights. The strongest predictors of low fertility across countries are female labor force participation, higher education attainment, urbanization, and cost of childrearing -- not LGBT acceptance.

## Key Points

- OECD panel data (1980-2009): same-sex marriage legalization has no negative effect on different-sex family formation [@trandafir2015]
- Netherlands diff-in-diff analysis: same-sex marriage (2001) did not reduce heterosexual marriage rates [@trandafir2013]
- Japan, South Korea, Poland have very low fertility but low-to-moderate LGBT acceptance
- Second demographic transition: declining fertility and rising LGBT acceptance share common structural drivers
- No causal evidence supports LGBT inclusion reducing birth rates
- Correlation is explained by shared underlying urbanization, education, and secularization trends
