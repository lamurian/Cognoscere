## Summary

Social scientists develop new assessment scales for several interrelated reasons. The most legitimate motivation is the absence of an existing instrument that adequately captures a specific construct in a particular context. Best practice guidelines explicitly instruct scale developers to "confirm that there are no existing instruments" before proceeding with new development, and to justify why a new instrument is appropriate when similar ones exist (@boateng2018).

However, less legitimate motivations also drive scale creation. The publish-or-perish incentive in academia makes new scale development an attractive path to publication, since developing and validating a new instrument constitutes a publishable contribution in its own right. This creates a structural incentive for proliferation. Additionally, scholars sometimes create new constructs from existing ones with only minor conceptual distinctions, leading to what @shaffer2015 term "construct proliferation" -- the accumulation of ostensibly different but potentially identical constructs.

Genuine measurement gaps do exist. New social phenomena (e.g., digital burnout, AI dependence, hybrid work well-being) may require purpose-built instruments. Cultural and contextual adaptation of existing scales may be insufficient when the underlying construct manifests differently across populations. Some existing instruments also lack adequate validity evidence or have known psychometric deficiencies.

## Key Points

- Best practice requires confirming no existing instrument serves the same purpose before developing a new one (@boateng2018)
- Academic incentive structures reward new scale development as a publishable output
- Construct proliferation -- creating near-identical constructs -- is a recognized problem in organizational and psychological research (@shaffer2015)
- New social phenomena sometimes genuinely require new measurement approaches
- Existing instruments may have inadequate validity evidence or poor psychometric properties
