## Summary

Trait resilience conceptualises resilience as a relatively stable personal quality — a disposition, personality characteristic, or set of innate attributes that predispose individuals to adapt effectively under adversity. The trait perspective originates in early ego-resiliency theory (Block & Block, 1986) and was operationalised through instruments such as the Ego-Resiliency Scale (ER89), the Dispositional Resilience Scale (DRS-15), and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), which measure resilience as a cluster of personal qualities rather than a dynamic process [@helmreich2017; @windle2011].

Trait resilience encompasses several theoretically distinct but empirically overlapping constructs. Hardiness — comprising commitment, control, and challenge — is measured by the DRS-15 and predicts health and performance under stress, though its psychometric profile is weaker than other instruments [@bartone2007; @windle2011]. Ego-resiliency (ER89/ER11) captures adaptive flexibility — the capacity to adjust behaviour to changing environmental demands — rather than adversity-specific recovery [@block1996; @farkas2015]. The CD-RISC, the most widely used resilience scale, also measures resilience as a set of personal characteristics (personal tenacity, strength, cognitively oriented optimism), conflating the resource-like qualities with the outcome of successful adaptation [@connor2003].

The trait perspective has been critiqued for several limitations. It implies resilience is fixed and relatively impervious to intervention, which contradicts evidence that resilience can be trained and developed [@southwick2014]. It conflates protective factors (inputs) with resilience itself (the adaptive outcome or process), creating what Helmreich et al. term a jingle-jangle problem — the same term used for different constructs and different terms for the same construct [@helmreich2017]. Denckla et al. (2020) argue that trait-based definitions fail to capture the context-dependent, dynamic nature of resilient functioning, which varies across situations, developmental stages, and cultural contexts [@denckla2020].

Farchi and Peled-Avram (2025) directly address the trait-versus-state question, arguing that what appear to be stable traits are better understood as dynamically accessible resources that become mobilised during the stress response. The ART framework (Acknowledgment, Reframe, Tailoring) positions trait-like personal resources — such as self-efficacy, optimism, and hardiness — as the pool from which individuals draw during adversity, but emphasises that the act of drawing from these resources is itself a dynamic, context-dependent process [@farchi2025]. Kuldas and Foody (2022) propose transactional resiliency/e as an alternative to both trait and state framings, arguing that resilience emerges from the dynamic transaction between person and environment rather than residing as a fixed attribute within the individual [@kuldas2021].

## Key Points

- Trait resilience = stable personality characteristic or disposition (Block's ego-resiliency, Bartone's hardiness, CD-RISC)
- Measured by ER89/ER11, DRS-15, CD-RISC — all treat resilience as personal qualities rather than process
- Critiqued for implying resilience is fixed, for conflating protective factors with resilience itself, and for ignoring context
- Trait-like resources become dynamically accessible during adversity (ART framework) rather than being simply activated
- Kuldas & Foody propose transactional resiliency/e as alternative to both trait and state framings
