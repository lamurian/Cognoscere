## Summary

The evidence supports mindfulness as a potentially important resilience-*supporting* process, but does not justify describing mindfulness as a foundational or fundamental determinant of resilience. This distinction is critical for scientific defensibility.

**What the evidence shows:**

Mitsea et al. [@mitsea2023] conducted a systematic review of 66 experimental studies involving 21,432 participants examining digitally assisted mindfulness interventions. The review found that mindfulness training consistently develops a wide range of self-regulation meta-skills: attention regulation, impulse control, cognitive control, emotional regulation, stress management, and resilience-related capacities. These findings demonstrate that mindfulness *improves self-regulation*.

**The evidence chain is:**

> mindfulness → improved self-regulation → self-regulation is plausibly relevant to adaptation under stress → therefore mindfulness *may* contribute to resilience.

This is scientifically reasonable. It is *not* the same as claiming:

> mindfulness → proven foundational resilience factor.

The latter requires stronger evidence than the Mitsea review provides. Contemporary resilience science increasingly emphasizes that resilience is not simply an individual psychological trait — organisational, interpersonal, and socioeconomic environments matter too [@kirkbride2024].

**Evidence for adaptive functioning under adversity:**

Li et al. [@li2024b] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of MBSR effectiveness for depression, PTSD, and mindfulness among military veterans (13 studies, 1131 participants). Both within- and between-group comparisons demonstrated reductions in depressive and PTSD symptoms with medium effect sizes. This provides useful evidence that mindfulness-based interventions can support adaptive functioning under adversity.

However, resilience is a broader construct than symptom reduction. It encompasses the capacity to adapt and recover across multiple domains — psychological, social, organisational, and structural.

**What the evidence does not establish:**

The Mitsea review demonstrates improved self-regulation. It does not demonstrate that mindfulness is a *foundational* resilience factor — that is, a basic building block upon which resilient lives are primarily constructed. Self-regulation is one component of a complex adaptive system that includes social support, economic stability, healthcare access, safe environments, and structural conditions.

**The structural determinants framework:**

Kirkbride et al. [@kirkbride2024], in a comprehensive review in *World Psychiatry*, conclude that socioeconomic disadvantage, discrimination, housing, food security, employment, childhood adversity, and social support have major effects on mental health. These structural factors affect both exposure to stress and people's ability to access protective resources and healthcare.

This gives an important refinement:

> Mindfulness can be universally applicable as a human capacity, while the conditions necessary to benefit from formal mindfulness interventions are not universally available.

Those propositions are compatible. Someone experiencing homelessness may be capable of mindfulness. But saying that person can simply "use mindfulness" to solve their distress would be scientifically and ethically inadequate. Conversely, saying their socioeconomic circumstances make mindfulness inherently irrelevant is also unsupported.

**The integrated model:**

The best model is multi-level:

Structural conditions → exposure to adversity and available resources → psychological processes (including attention, self-regulation) → adaptive response.

Mindfulness potentially occupies one part of that pathway — the psychological processes level. It does not control the entire pathway.

**The revised thesis:**

> Mindfulness is a broadly accessible human capacity and a potentially important resilience-supporting process, but its benefits are neither universal nor sufficient on their own. Its effectiveness depends on individual, cultural, clinical, and socioeconomic context, while structural determinants of mental health must be addressed alongside individual psychological skills.

This formulation preserves the core insight that mindfulness is not inherently a privilege of the affluent, while also accommodating the strongest criticism that access, implementation, and structural conditions matter. It avoids the evidential leap from "mindfulness improves self-regulation" to "mindfulness is the fundamental basis of resilient mental health."
