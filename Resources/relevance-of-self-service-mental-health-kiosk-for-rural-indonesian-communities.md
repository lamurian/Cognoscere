## Summary

The proposed Interactive Psychological Immediate Coping Aid kiosk directly addresses four documented barriers to mental health access in rural Indonesia. (1) Geographical barriers: rural populations travel 60+ km to psychiatric units [@munira2023]. A kiosk placed in villages eliminates this travel burden. (2) Stigma: kiosk-based screening is private and anonymous. Wrenn et al. found patients disclose more to computers than humans [@wrenn2015]. The mPareshan study confirmed technology is perceived as 'modern and confidential,' reducing stigma [@akhtar2025]. (3) Workforce shortages: Indonesia has 0.3 psychiatrists per 100,000 population vs WHO-recommended 3.3 [@anjara2019]. A self-service kiosk doesn't require specialist staff. (4) Referral failures: up to 90% of referrals never result in appointments, GPs refuse referral letters [@munira2023]. An automated printed referral ticket bypasses these gatekeeping barriers.

The kiosk's offline-first design is critical. Only 20% of Puskesmas provide mental health services [@hidayat2023], and many villages lack reliable internet [@solarkiosksolutionsgmbh2023]. A solar-powered, offline-first kiosk can function where digital infrastructure is absent, syncing data when connectivity returns. The WHO PFA framework [@worldhealthorganization2011] provides the evidence base for immediate coping aid, while Indonesian-validated screening tools (PHQ-9, GAD-7) can be administered via touchscreen.

Risk classification (ringan/sedang/berat) aligns with the stepped-care model recommended for LMICs: mild cases receive self-help/PFA content from the kiosk, moderate cases receive kiosk-guided coping plus Puskesmas referral, severe cases receive urgent referral with printed ticket. The system must integrate with existing Puskesmas referral pathways [@surjaningrum2018] rather than creating parallel structures.

Key success factors: integration with existing community health infrastructure (kader/Puskesmas), culturally adapted audio-visual content in Bahasa Indonesia, privacy guarantees to overcome stigma, and clear clinical governance for the risk classification algorithm.

## Key Points
- Addresses 4 barriers: geography (60+ km travel), stigma (computer more confidential), workforce (0.3 psychiatrists/100k), referral failures (90% never seen)
- Offline-first design essential for villages without reliable internet
- Solar power enables deployment in off-grid locations
- WHO PFA (2011) provides evidence for immediate coping aid component
- Risk classification (ringan/sedang/berat) aligns with stepped-care LMIC model
- Printed referral ticket bypasses GP gatekeeping documented in Munira et al.
- Must integrate with existing Puskesmas/kader referral pathways, not create parallel system
- Culturally adapted content needed: avoid psychiatric labels, use local idioms
