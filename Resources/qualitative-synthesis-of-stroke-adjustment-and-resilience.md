## Summary

Sarre et al. (2013) conducted a systematic review of 40 qualitative studies on adjusting after stroke (1990-2011), synthesising findings from stroke survivors' and carers' perspectives and considering their contribution to understanding resilience.[@sarre2013]

The review found that the impact of stroke was felt across multiple dimensions of experience, with permeable boundaries between physical, cognitive, emotional, and social domains. Adjustment was not temporally bounded but marked by setbacks and new challenges over time.[@sarre2013]

**Personal, interpersonal, and structural resources.** Participants identified personal characteristics as key to adjustment, but also employed practical and mental strategies. Relationships (including with healthcare professionals) and structural factors (access to health services, employment, welfare systems) also influenced adjustment.[@sarre2013]

**Lessons for resilience research.** The authors argued that the temporal unfolding of stroke adjustment presents a new challenge for resilience research, which has typically focused on discrete adverse events. The reviewed studies pointed to the importance of an emic perspective on adversity, social support, and what constitutes a "good" outcome when researching resilience, as well as the need for greater focus on embodiment.[@sarre2013]

**Implications for rehabilitation.** Stroke can cause profound effects on survivors' sense of self and relationships, not just physical functioning. Rehabilitation should account for the interconnected physical, cognitive, and psychosocial dimensions of adjustment.[@sarre2013]

## Key Points

- 40 qualitative studies synthesised (1990-2011)
- Stroke adjustment unfolds across physical, cognitive, emotional, social dimensions
- Adjustment is not temporally bounded; marked by ongoing setbacks
- Personal characteristics, practical strategies, relationships, and structural factors all contribute
- Resilience research needs emic perspectives and focus on embodiment
