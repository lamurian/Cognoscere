## Summary

Resilience as process conceptualises resilience not as a fixed trait or a static outcome, but as the dynamic, adaptive interaction between individual and environment that unfolds over time during and after adversity. This is the dominant contemporary view in resilience science, favoured because it captures the context-dependent, fluctuating, and modifiable nature of resilient functioning [@helmreich2017; @sisto2019; @stainton2018].

The process perspective defines resilience as the effective negotiation of, adaptation to, or management of significant sources of stress or trauma, facilitated by assets and resources within the individual, their life, and their environment [@southwick2014; @windle2011]. Kalisch et al. (2015) frame resilience as the operation of an internal resilience mechanism — a process of assessing a stressful situation, activating an adaptive strategy (positive reappraisal, emotion regulation, cognitive reframing), and implementing it through neural circuits involving the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, and anterior cingulate cortex. When this mechanism operates successfully, the individual maintains stable functioning despite adversity — the hallmark of resilience [@kalisch2014].

A critical feature of the resilience process is adaptive strategy selection. Resilient individuals are not those who deploy a single coping strategy but those who flexibly select the most efficient strategy to match the specific demands of the situation. Troy et al. (2023) articulate this through an affect-regulation framework: resilience depends on flexible deployment of regulatory strategies matched to stressor characteristics — cognitive reappraisal is more effective for controllable stressors, while acceptance is more effective for uncontrollable ones. Inflexible use of any single strategy, even an adaptive one, produces poorer outcomes. This flexibility constitutes the "dynamic adaptive" character of the resilience process [@troy2023].

The ART framework (Farchi & Peled-Avram, 2025) provides a three-stage model of the resilience process: (1) Acknowledgment — recognition of available coping resources and acceptance of the situation; (2) Reframe — reinterpretation of threats as challenges; (3) Tailoring — matching available resources to specific situational demands. Each stage involves cognitive, emotional, and physiological mechanisms working together. The framework bridges trait and process perspectives by showing how trait-like personal resources become dynamically accessible during the stress response through these three stages. The process is not a one-time activation but a continuous, flexible operation that adjusts in real time as circumstances change [@farchi2025].

The resilience process is also characterised by its multisystem nature. Masten et al. (2021) argue that resilience depends on interactions across individual, family, community, cultural, and ecological systems — the process is distributed, not localised within a single individual. This explains why the same individual may show resilient functioning in one ecological context but not another: the process depends on the match between individual resources and environmental demands at a given moment. Stainton et al. (2018) synthesise this into a multimodal dynamic process model integrating cognitive, emotional, social, and physiological subsystems, each operating on different timescales but interacting to produce resilient outcomes [@masten2021; @stainton2018].

The process perspective has important implications for intervention. Because resilience is a dynamic, modifiable process rather than a fixed trait, it can be strengthened through training. Abate et al. (2024) report pooled effect sizes for resilience-promoting interventions: mixed interventions d = 0.51, resilience-specific interventions d = 0.52, CBT interventions d = 0.30, and mindfulness-based interventions d = 0.21. The Cochrane protocol by Helmreich et al. (2017) explicitly frames resilience interventions as strengthening modifiable resilience factors rather than altering fixed personality traits, consistent with the process conceptualisation [@abate2024; @helmreich2017].

## Key Points

- Resilience process = dynamic, adaptive interaction between individual and environment during adversity
- Mechanism: assess situation -> activate adaptive strategy -> implement through neural circuits (Kalisch 2015)
- Adaptive strategy selection: flexible matching of coping strategy to stressor demands (Troy 2023)
- ART framework: Acknowledge -> Reframe -> Tailore — three stages of dynamic resource mobilisation (Farchi 2025)
- Multisystem: individual, family, community, cultural, ecological interactions (Masten 2021)
- Modifiable and trainable: interventions produce d = 0.30-0.52 improvements (Abate 2024)
