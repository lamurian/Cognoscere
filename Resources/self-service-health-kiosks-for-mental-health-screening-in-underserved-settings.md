## Summary

A quality improvement study by Wrenn et al. (2015) demonstrated the feasibility of using a self-service health kiosk placed in a primary care clinic waiting room to screen for multiple mental health disorders [@wrenn2015]. The kiosk administered validated screening instruments (PHQ-9 for depression, MDQ for bipolar disorder, PTSD-PC for PTSD, DAST-10/AUDIT for substance use) via a touchscreen interface in an underserved urban clinic serving a predominantly African-American, low-income population.

Over the pilot period, 281 screens were completed, identifying positive screens for depression (30%), bipolar disorder (17%), PTSD, and hazardous substance use. Patients printed their results to give to their provider. The study found that patients are more likely to disclose sensitive information to a computer than to a person, supporting the privacy argument for self-service kiosks. The authors concluded that self-service kiosks represent 'a promising approach to integrated care' that uses patient waiting time productively [@wrenn2015].

The University of Vermont's Leahy Institute for Rural Partnerships is developing similar self-service health screening kiosks for rural communities, placing them in general stores, community centers, libraries, and schools to meet people 'at their point of need.' This demonstrates growing recognition that self-service health kiosks can bridge access gaps in underserved areas.

## Key Points
- Wrenn et al. (2015): 281 screens completed in underserved primary care clinic
- Touchscreen kiosk administered PHQ-9, MDQ, PTSD-PC, AUDIT, DAST-10
- 30% positive for depression; patients more willing to disclose to computer
- Patients printed results for provider, enabling integration with care
- UVM's rural health kiosk project shows same concept being applied for rural communities
- Directly relevant precedent for the proposed mental health screening kiosk
