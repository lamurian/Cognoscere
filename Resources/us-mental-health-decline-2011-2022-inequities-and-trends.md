## Summary

A Harvard Chan School study published in JAMA Network Open found that poor mental health among US adults rose significantly and inequitably between 2011 and 2022. Using three nationally representative surveys — the BRFSS, NSDUH, and NHIS — researchers documented that adults reporting poor mental health increased from 35.7% to 42.5% (BRFSS), 31.1% to 35.8% (NSDUH), and 18.7% to 20.5% (NHIS) over the study period [@hamad2025].

The rise was not evenly distributed. Younger adults consistently experienced worse mental health than older adults. Women reported worse mental health than men throughout the entire period. Racial dynamics shifted: Black adults experienced worse mental health than white adults until 2020, after which patterns changed — likely reflecting both COVID-19 impacts and evolving social stressors. The researchers noted that these findings align with prior work on US mental health trends but called for further research on mechanisms before and since 2020 to inform policy and clinical interventions.

Pre-pandemic, deaths of despair — from opioids and suicides — had already claimed over one million lives in just over a decade, concentrated among less-than-college-educated middle-aged whites who experienced income and social cohesion declines as manufacturing jobs disappeared. The pandemic exacerbated these pre-existing vulnerabilities, with EMS calls for drug overdoses nearly doubling and mental health-related calls increasing by roughly 30% in early 2020 compared to prior years [@graham2020].

## Key Points

- Poor mental health in US adults rose from ~36% to ~43% (BRFSS) between 2011-2022
- Consistent upward trend across three independent national surveys
- Younger adults, women, and racial minorities disproportionately affected
- Deaths of despair (opioids, suicides) predated the pandemic
- COVID-19 exacerbated existing vulnerabilities
- EMS calls for drug overdoses nearly doubled in early 2020
