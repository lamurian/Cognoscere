## Summary

Existing ethical frameworks for evaluating proctoring systems have emerged primarily in higher education contexts, but their application to psychometric assessment requires adaptation. Coghlan et al. articulate four key ethical principles for online exam supervision: transparency, informed consent, information minimisation, and accountability [@coghlan2021]. Mutimukwe et al. similarly identify five governing principles — transparency and fairness, consent and choice, information minimisation, information security and accuracy, and accountability — but note that the roles and responsibilities of different actors (students, institutions, technology companies, regulators) remain ambiguous [@mutimukwe2023].

A critical ethical concern is the lack of genuine informed consent. Coghlan et al. argue that consent cannot be considered genuine if test-takers cannot refuse proctoring without penalty — a situation that applies directly to many digital psychometric assessment contexts [@coghlan2021]. Most proctoring system descriptions provide insufficient detail on data processing, storage, and AI decision-making for test-takers to make truly informed decisions [@heinrich2025].

Henry and Oliver argue that current ethical principles risk becoming "ethical washing" that serves technology companies' interests rather than protecting test-takers [@henry2021]. They call for a more relational understanding of accountability that considers power imbalances between institutions, technology providers, and test-takers. The Heinrich review proposes open standards for proctoring identity profiles and flagging systems that would give test-takers control over their data [@heinrich2025].

For psychometric assessment specifically, additional governance requirements should include (a) evidence that proctoring does not change the psychometric properties of the instrument, (b) algorithmic impact assessments before deployment, (c) genuine opt-out alternatives, and (d) data sovereignty principles giving test-takers control over their biometric and response data.

## Key Points

- Existing ethical frameworks for proctoring require adaptation for psychometric contexts
- Genuine informed consent is practically impossible when proctoring is mandatory
- Current principles risk becoming "ethical washing" without enforcement mechanisms
- Open standards and data sovereignty could improve ethical governance
