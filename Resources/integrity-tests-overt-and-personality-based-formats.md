## Summary

Integrity tests are pre-employment assessments designed to predict counterproductive work behavior (CWB) — theft, absenteeism, disciplinary problems, workplace aggression — and overall job performance. Schmidt and Hunter (1998) reported a criterion-related validity of r = .41 for predicting job performance. When paired with a GMA test, integrity tests produce a multiple R of .65 — the strongest two-method combination in the meta-analytic literature.

### Two Types

**Overt integrity tests** (clear-purpose) ask directly about attitudes toward dishonesty, theft, and rule-breaking. They typically contain two sections: an attitudes section (e.g., "How often do you think the average employee takes things from work?") and an admissions section (e.g., "In the last three years, how much merchandise have you taken from an employer without permission?"). Well-known examples include the Personnel Reaction Blank (PRB), the Reid Report, and the Stanton Survey.

**Personality-based integrity tests** (disguised-purpose) measure personality traits empirically linked to counterproductive behavior — primarily conscientiousness, agreeableness, and emotional stability — without directly asking about dishonesty. Examples include the Hogan Reliability Scale and the Employee Reliability Inventory.

### Scoring and Interpretation

Integrity tests are scored algorithmically, with items weighted based on their empirical relationship to CWB criteria. Higher scores indicate greater integrity and lower risk of counterproductive behavior. There are no universal clinical cutoffs; scores are interpreted relative to applicant pools or organisational norms. The OPM notes that integrity tests show few subgroup differences by gender or race, making them low-adverse-impact selection tools.

### Validity Evidence

The foundational meta-analysis by Ones, Viswesvaran, and Schmidt (1993) analyzed 665 validity coefficients from over 500,000 test-takers, finding operational validity of .41 for predicting job performance and .47 for predicting counterproductive behaviors. Integrity tests correlate substantially with conscientiousness (r = .39), agreeableness (r = .40), and emotional stability (r = .33), but predict job performance incrementally over Big Five measures alone. Lau et al. (2023) confirmed mean validity of r = .43 for predicting workplace deviance across 150 studies.

## Key Points

- Two types: overt (direct questions about dishonesty) and personality-based (disguised purpose)
- Validity of r = .41 for job performance, .47 for counterproductive behaviors (Ones et al., 1993)
- GMA + integrity = R = .65 — strongest two-method selection combination
- Low adverse impact: minimal group differences by race or gender
- Correlates with conscientiousness (r=.39), agreeableness (r=.40), emotional stability (r=.33)
- Faking is a concern but does not eliminate predictive validity
