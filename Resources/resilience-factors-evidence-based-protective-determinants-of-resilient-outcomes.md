## Summary

Resilience factors are the empirically validated personal, social, and environmental determinants that predict the likelihood of achieving a resilient outcome under adversity. They are the inputs — the resources, capacities, and conditions — that enable individuals to maintain or recover adaptive functioning during stress. Critically, resilience factors are conceptually distinct from both trait resilience (the stable personality characteristic) and resilience itself (the outcome or process of successful adaptation) [@helmreich2017; @southwick2014].

Helmreich et al. (2017) catalogue well-evidenced psychosocial resilience factors rated by level of evidence. Level 1 factors (strong evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses across populations) include: active coping (problem-solving, planning), self-efficacy, optimism or positive attributional style, social support, cognitive flexibility (positive reappraisal, acceptance), and religiosity/spirituality. Level 1b factors include positive emotions, hardiness, and self-esteem. Level 1c factors include meaning/purpose in life and sense of coherence. Level 2 factors (moderate evidence) include locus of control, coping flexibility, hope, and humour. These factors overlap but are not identical with trait resilience constructs — hardiness is both a trait measure (DRS-15) and a resilience factor, while self-efficacy is a resilience factor measured by separate instruments [@helmreich2017].

The umbrella review by Abate et al. (2024), synthesising 44 systematic reviews (N = 556,920), quantifies the pooled effect sizes of specific resilience factor categories on resilience development after adversity: self-regulation (d = 0.43), positive self-perception (d = 0.34), availability of support (d = 0.26), religious involvement (d = 0.23), cognitive ability (d = 0.09), and community cohesion (d = 0.05). The overall pooled effect of all protective factors was d = 0.31 (p < 0.001), confirming that resilience factors meaningfully predict resilient outcomes [@abate2024].

The conceptual demarcation between resilience factors and resilience itself is critical. Resources represent inputs or antecedents that exist prior to adversity and predict which trajectory an individual follows, while resilience is the process or observed outcome of successful adaptation under adversity. Pellerin et al. (2022) demonstrated this empirically: psychological resources (wisdom, optimism, hope, self-efficacy) measured at baseline predicted membership in resilient versus symptomatic trajectories, mediated by psychological flexibility. This three-part causal chain — resources enable flexibility, which produces resilience — preserves the conceptual distinction between factors (inputs) and resilience (outcome) [@pellerin2022].

The Schäfer et al. (2023) network analysis reveals that resilience factors form a correlated network where sense of coherence (SOC) is the most central node, integrating multiple psychological resources. The strongest partial correlations among factors were SOC with sense of mastery (r = 0.35), dispositional resilience with self-efficacy (r = 0.34), and coping using emotional support with social support (r = 0.37). This network structure suggests that resilience factors do not operate in isolation but interact dynamically, reinforcing each other and collectively enabling resilient functioning [@schfer2023].

## Key Points

- Resilience factors = empirically validated inputs (personal, social, environmental) that predict resilient outcomes
- Level 1 evidence: active coping, self-efficacy, optimism, social support, cognitive flexibility, religiosity/spirituality
- Pooled effect sizes: self-regulation d = 0.43, positive self-perception d = 0.34, support d = 0.26 (Abate et al. 2024)
- Conceptually distinct from trait resilience and from resilience itself (factors are inputs; resilience is process/outcome)
- Resources -> flexibility -> resilience: three-part causal chain (Pellerin et al. 2022)
- Resilience factors form a correlated network with SOC as the most central node (Schäfer et al. 2023)
