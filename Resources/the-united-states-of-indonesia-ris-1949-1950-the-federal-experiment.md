## Summary

Yes, Indonesia has been a federal state once. On 27 December 1949 the Netherlands formally transferred sovereignty over the Dutch East Indies to the United States of Indonesia (Republik Indonesia Serikat, RIS), ending the four-year independence war [@wikipediacontributors2025g]. The RIS was a federation of sixteen entities: seven states (negara) — including the Republic of Indonesia itself, reduced to parts of Java and Sumatra — plus nine autonomous territories. Apart from the Republic, every constituent had been established by the Dutch, mostly between 1946 and 1949, as part of a strategy to rebuild control after the Japanese occupation [@wikipediacontributors2025g].

The federal structure came out of the Dutch-Indonesian Round Table Conference in The Hague, where the Dutch and UN delegates helped draft the 1949 Federal Constitution. Federalism had no deep domestic roots: Indonesian nationalists saw it as a Dutch divide-and-rule device to weaken the new nation, and the framers of the 1945 Constitution had explicitly rejected it only four years earlier [@wikipediacontributors2025g; @omara2022].

From the outset most Indonesians opposed the system — it was associated with colonialism, feared because it might let states secede, and its component states were led by traditional rulers seen as pro-Dutch. Within months the constituent states dissolved themselves one by one into the Republic, and on 17 August 1950 President Sukarno formally replaced the RIS with the unitary Republic of Indonesia, as the federated governments "unanimously decided" to revert to a unitary form [@wikipediacontributors2025g; @encyclopaediabritannica2026]. Bertrand notes a unitarist "sweep" within eight months: virtually all federal states voluntarily disbanded and joined the Republic [@bertrand2019].

## Key Points

- The RIS existed from 27 December 1949 to 17 August 1950 — under eight months.
- It comprised 7 states and 9 autonomous entities; all but the Republic were Dutch creations.
- Federalism was perceived as a colonial divide-and-rule instrument, tainting any future federal proposal.
- The dissolution was driven by popular pressure and the states themselves, not by force.
