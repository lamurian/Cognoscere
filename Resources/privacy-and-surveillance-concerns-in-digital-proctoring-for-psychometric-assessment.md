## Summary

Digital proctoring systems used to monitor test-takers during remote assessments collect a wide range of sensitive personal data far beyond what is typical in supervised in-person testing. A scoping review by Mutimukwe et al. identified three categories of collected information: individual identification data (names, IDs, addresses), device monitoring data (screen images, browser history, keystrokes, IP addresses), and AI-based biometric data (facial and voice recognition, eye tracking, 360-degree room scans) [@mutimukwe2023]. For digital self-administered psychometric instruments, this level of surveillance is particularly concerning because these assessments often measure sensitive psychological constructs where privacy expectations are high.

Students and test-takers report significant discomfort with continuous monitoring through webcams and microphones, especially when assessments are taken in personal living spaces [@coghlan2021]. The room scan feature, which requires test-takers to show their physical environment via webcam, has been deemed unconstitutional by a US federal judge as an unreasonable search [@linkletter2026]. For psychometric assessment, this surveillance may increase test anxiety and alter responses, threatening the validity of the measurement itself.

The principle of information minimization — collecting only data necessary for the assessment purpose — is frequently violated by proctoring systems. Many platforms store data for extended periods and share it with third-party companies, raising risks of unauthorized access, data breaches, and secondary use without consent [@mutimukwe2023; @coghlan2021].

## Key Points

- Proctoring systems collect three categories of data: identity, device monitoring, and biometric/AI-analysed data
- Room scans and continuous surveillance represent privacy invasions that may be illegal in some jurisdictions
- Psychometric testing creates heightened privacy expectations compared to educational exams
- Data retention and third-party sharing practices lack transparency and informed consent
