## Summary

The tension between generalizability and authoring new scales is a fundamental methodological concern. @houts2022 argue that scale development is a complex activity requiring significant investments, and that validity theory and psychometrics can support expanding the uses of existing scales rather than creating new ones. This approach prioritizes generalizability by building a cumulative evidence base for established instruments.

When researchers develop new scales, they often validate them in narrow samples (typically university students), limiting generalizability from the outset. In contrast, existing scales that have been validated across multiple populations provide stronger evidence for transportability. @boateng2018 note that measurement invariance testing -- determining whether a scale functions the same way across groups -- is essential for generalizability, yet this step is frequently omitted in new scale development studies.

@shaffer2015 emphasize that construct proliferation undermines generalizability by creating confusion about what is actually being measured. When multiple instruments exist for ostensibly the same construct, researchers must choose among them without clear guidance about which provides the most valid and generalizable measurements. This choice can substantially affect research findings and their applicability.

## Key Points

- Expanding uses of existing scales is often preferable to developing new ones (@houts2022)
- New scales are typically validated in narrow samples, limiting initial generalizability
- Existing scales validated across multiple populations offer stronger transportability evidence
- Measurement invariance testing is essential but often omitted in new development (@boateng2018)
- Construct proliferation creates confusion about measurement choices and hinders generalizability (@shaffer2015)
