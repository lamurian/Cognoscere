## Summary

The boundary of social construction is drawn by Searle's distinction between brute facts and institutional facts. Brute facts — that a mountain is here, that water is H2O — exist independently of human institutions and collective acceptance. Institutional facts — that this paper is money, that this person is president — exist only because a community collectively accepts status functions [@searle1995; @mallon2024]. Whether an object falls inside or outside the domain of social construction thus depends on whether its existence and character are metaphysically tied to collective social-conceptual activity.

Global constructionism — the claim that every fact is socially constructed — fails at the boundary. It faces a regress problem: the thesis itself would have to be a social construction, undermining its own claim to truth [@boghossian2006; @mallon2024]. The viable claims are local ones. For covert constructions, Boghossian raises the decisive objection: "isn't it part of the very concept of an electron, or of a mountain, that these things were not constructed by us?" [@boghossian2006]. Constructionists answer by adopting a causal theory of reference: terms like "race" can refer to a kind produced by socio-linguistic behavior even if that fact is only revealed a posteriori, just as "water" referred to H2O before modern chemistry [@mallon2024].

Two further boundary rules keep the concept honest. First, the reformation fallacy: from "X is socially constructed" it does not follow that X is easy to change — race and gender are constructed yet sustained by interlocking networks of belief and practice; from "X is natural" it does not follow that X cannot change — poor eyesight is biological yet easily fixed with glasses [@diazleon2015]. Second, changeability tracks the causal/constitutive distinction: constitutive constructions change directly when social practices change (reform the practice and the status disappears), while causal constructions have metaphysical independence from the forces that produced them (a wristwatch survives the collapse of the society that made it) [@diazleon2015; @mallon2024].

## Key Points

- Boundary: dependence on collective acceptance — brute facts outside, institutional facts inside [@searle1995]
- Global constructionism collapses into regress; only local claims survive [@boghossian2006; @mallon2024]
- Boghossian's objection: concepts like electron and mountain presuppose independence from us [@boghossian2006]
- Causal theory of reference shows covert constructions can be real but socially grounded [@mallon2024]
- Reformation fallacy: constructed does not mean easy to reform; natural does not mean unchangeable [@diazleon2015]
- Constitutive constructions change with practices; causal constructions do not [@diazleon2015]
