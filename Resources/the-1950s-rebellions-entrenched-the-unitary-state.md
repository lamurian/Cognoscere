## Summary

Almost as soon as the unitary state was restored in 1950, regional revolts hardened the commitment to centralisation. The first was the South Moluccas Republic (RMS) secession attempt in 1950, launched after the fall of the Dutch-sponsored federal state. The second was Darul Islam, an Islamist rebellion that rejected the Pancasila state and lasted into the early 1960s, rooted in South Sulawesi, West Java, and Aceh. Regional resentment at over-centralisation also produced the PRRI rebellion of 1958 in parts of Sumatra and Sulawesi, which sought an alternative government [@bertrand2019].

These challenges shaped Indonesia's institutions. The instability of the 1950s drove Soekarno and the political elite toward strong centralisation; in 1959 he suspended the Constituent Assembly elected in 1955 (deadlocked between nationalist and Islamist visions) and decreed a return to the 1945 Constitution, inaugurating Guided Democracy [@bertrand2019]. Under the New Order (1965–1998), Suharto deepened centralisation further, stripping the autonomy regional governments had once enjoyed and banning political mobilisation along ethnic, religious, or regional (SARA) lines [@bertrand2019; @bennet2010].

Bertrand's summary is blunt: from the brief RIS episode “federalism was subsequently shunned and virtually banished as a possible option to accommodate Indonesia's diversity.” Indonesian leaders instead pursued an almost obsessive emphasis on unity and centralisation, framed by the motto Bhinneka Tunggal Ika (“unity in diversity”) [@bertrand2019].

## Key Points

- RMS (1950), Darul Islam (until early 1960s), and PRRI (1958) followed the return to unitary rule.
- The 1959 decree returning to the 1945 Constitution cemented the unitary state under Guided Democracy.
- The New Order outlawed SARA-based (ethnic/religious/regional) political mobilisation.
- Federalism became effectively taboo — "virtually banished" as an option.
