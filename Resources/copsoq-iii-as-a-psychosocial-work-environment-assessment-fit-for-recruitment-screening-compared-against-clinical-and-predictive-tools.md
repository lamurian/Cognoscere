## Summary

The Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQ III) is fundamentally different from the clinical screening tools (PHQ-9, GAD-7, K10) and predictive assessments (Big Five, GMA tests) cited previously. COPSOQ III measures psychosocial work environment conditions, not individual mental health symptoms or individual psychological traits. This distinction determines its limited suitability as a standalone recruitment screening tool, but its strong value as a complementary instrument in the recruitment process.

COPSOQ III was developed at the Danish National Research Centre for the Working Environment and has been validated in over 25 languages across multiple countries. The third version (2019) contains 26 psychosocial scales grouped into six domains: Work Demands (quantitative demands, work pace, cognitive demands, emotional demands, demands for hiding emotions), Work Organisation (influence at work, possibilities for development, meaning of work, commitment to workplace), Interpersonal Relations and Leadership (role clarity, role conflicts, quality of leadership, social support from supervisor and colleagues, sense of community), Work-Individual Interface (job insecurity, work-life conflict, job satisfaction, trust in management), and Offensive Behaviours (harassment, bullying, sexual harassment, violence). The instrument is available in long (26 scales), middle, and short versions, and has been cited by the WHO, ILO, and EU-OSHA as a reference tool for psychosocial risk assessment.

## How COPSOQ III Works

COPSOQ III operates on a cause-effect model: psychosocial working conditions (stressors/resources) lead to psychological and physiological strain responses. The 26 scales measure both risk factors (negative scales where high score = hazard, e.g., quantitative demands, emotional demands) and protective factors (positive scales where high score = resource, e.g., influence at work, social support). Scores are benchmarked against population reference values to classify results as favourable, intermediate, or unfavourable.

The instrument maps to all 17 Safe Work Australia psychosocial hazard categories and is aligned with ISO 45003 requirements for psychosocial risk management. This makes it the most comprehensive single instrument for workplace-level psychosocial hazard identification.

## Why COPSOQ III Does Not Fit Well as a Recruitment Screening Tool

Three fundamental reasons limit COPSOQ III's suitability for individual-level recruitment screening:

**1. Wrong unit of analysis.** COPSOQ III measures work environment, not individual mental health. A candidate completing COPSOQ III would report on a workplace they have not yet experienced, which produces meaningless individual-level data. The instrument requires respondents to have actual workplace experience to provide valid responses.

**2. No individual-level diagnostic thresholds.** Unlike PHQ-9 (cutoff >=10 for depression) or GAD-7 (cutoff >=10 for anxiety), COPSOQ III does not provide individual-level cutoff scores for mental health conditions. It provides population-level benchmarks for group-level risk assessment, not individual clinical decisions.

**3. Designed for existing employees, not candidates.** COPSOQ III's guidelines specify it should be used within a participatory process involving management and workers' representatives. It is designed for ongoing organisational development, not one-time pre-employment assessment.

## Where COPSOQ III Adds Value in Recruitment

Despite not being a direct recruitment screening tool, COPSOQ III serves two important roles:

**Role 1: Pre-hire job analysis.** Before recruiting for a position, employers can administer COPSOQ III to existing employees in similar roles to assess psychosocial demands. This provides candidates with realistic job previews and informs accommodation planning. If COPSOQ III reveals high emotional demands, low influence, or poor social support, the employer can proactively plan onboarding support.

**Role 2: Post-hire baseline measurement.** COPSOQ III administered at onboarding establishes a psychosocial baseline against which future changes can be measured. This supports duty of care obligations and enables early identification of emerging risk factors.
