## Summary

Baba Malay is a creole of Malay and Hokkien spoken by descendants of Chinese settlers in the Straits Settlements (Penang, Malacca, Singapore). The term 'Baba' refers to Straits Chinese men, originating as a Hindi/Persian loanword adopted by Malay speakers as a term of affection. The Peranakan Chinese commonly call themselves Baba-Nyonya. This linguistic tradition provides a useful comparison for understanding the Chinese-Malay contact situation in Batavia/Jakarta.

Research on Baba Malay shows that Hokkien loanwords form a significant portion of the vocabulary, with many words entering through daily domestic and commercial interaction. The phonological adaptation of Hokkien words follows patterns visible in Betawi: initial consonant shifts, vowel modifications, and tonal loss. For example, Hokkien 我 (góa) becomes *gua* in both Baba Malay and Betawi, suggesting parallel borrowing processes from the same source dialect.

The connection between Betawi's Chinese elements and the broader Peranakan Chinese world is significant: Chinese men who married local women (Balinese, Javanese, Sundanese) in Batavia created a mixed community whose speech patterns contributed to Betawi's development. This parallels the formation of Baba communities in the Straits, where Chinese-Malay intermarriage produced distinct creole traditions.

## Key Points

- Baba Malay: Hokkien-Malay creole in Straits Settlements (Penang, Malacca, Singapore)
- 'Baba' = Straits Chinese men; 'Nyonya' = Straits Chinese women
- Hokkien loanwords enter through domestic and commercial contact
- Parallel borrowing patterns: Hokkien 我 (góa) → *gua* in both Baba Malay and Betawi
- Chinese-local intermarriage in Batavia created mixed community contributing to Betawi
