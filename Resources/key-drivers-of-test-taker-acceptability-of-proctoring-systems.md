## Summary

Jiang et al. (2023) surveyed 760 Chinese university students using an extended Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and found that social influence was the strongest predictor of online proctoring acceptance (beta = 0.442), followed by perceived usefulness (beta = 0.259) and social presence (beta = 0.236). Social influence refers to government policy, school organisation, and teacher endorsement [@jiang2023]. Surprisingly, perceived ease of use had a significant NEGATIVE effect on acceptance (beta = -0.168) — when technology requirements were easily met, ease of use did not drive acceptance. Place presence (immersive feel of traditional exams) had no significant effect.

Denker et al. (2023) analysed 2,052 test-takers across six aptitude tests and found that subject specificity strongly predicted acceptance: the more specific the test content to the study programme (e.g., pharmacy vs. general mental ability), the higher the face validity (beta = 0.43) and overall evaluation (beta = 0.33). Test duration negatively affected all acceptance dimensions. Prior information was a strong predictor of controllability (beta = 0.29), face validity (beta = 0.27), and overall evaluation (beta = 0.18) [@denker2023].

The Andreou et al. (2021) qualitative analysis of 304 candidates revealed four themes: (1) stress/anxiety before the exam, (2) stress during the exam, (3) anxiety specifically about the proctoring software (feeling watched, fear of false flagging), and (4) positive reassurance from knowing help was available. Some candidates appreciated the direct connection with the university [@andreou2021].

Key drivers can be summarised as: (1) institutional endorsement (social influence), (2) perceived usefulness (flexibility, convenience, integrity), (3) prior information (transparency about procedures), (4) test specificity (face validity of content), (5) social presence (seeing familiar faces), and (6) absence of technical issues. Key barriers are: privacy concerns (~10% of test-takers), fear of false accusations, test anxiety amplification, and long test duration.

## Key Points

- Social influence (government/school endorsement) is the strongest predictor of acceptance (beta = 0.44)
- Perceived usefulness matters more than ease of use for experienced technology users
- Prior information and test specificity strongly predict acceptance
- ~10% of test-takers consistently report privacy concerns
