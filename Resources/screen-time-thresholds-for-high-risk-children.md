## Summary

There is no single universally agreed-upon hourly cutoff for 'high' screen time in children, but multiple evidence-based thresholds emerge from pediatric research. The WHO (2019) recommends no screen time for children under 2, a maximum of 1 hour per day for children aged 3-5, and limited use for older children. The AAP's 2026 guidance retired fixed hourly limits for children over 6, replacing them with a context-based '5 C's' framework. However, epidemiological evidence points to specific thresholds where risk becomes clinically meaningful.

For cardiometabolic risk, the Horner et al. @horner2025 study found that every additional hour of recreational screen time was associated with measurable increases in cardiometabolic risk scores: +0.08 standard deviations per hour for 10-year-olds and +0.13 SD per hour for 18-year-olds. The authors noted that 'a child with three extra hours of screen time a day would have roughly a quarter to half a standard-deviation higher risk than their peers.' At 3-6 hours of daily recreational screen time, which is common among adolescents, risk accumulates to levels that matter at a population level. The CDC reports that US children aged 8-10 average 6 hours per day, while 11-14 year-olds average 9 hours.

For neurodevelopmental outcomes, an NIH study found that children with more than 7 hours of daily screen time experienced measurable thinning of the brain's cortex, the region responsible for critical thinking and reasoning. For 1-year-olds, exposure to more than 4 hours of screen time per day was associated with developmental delays at ages 2 and 4 @takahashi2023. These thresholds are not safety boundaries but rather points where research has detected statistically significant harm.

## Key Points

- **Under 18 months**: 0 hours (AAP/WHO) -- video chatting is the sole exception
- **18-24 months**: limited use with co-viewing of high-quality content
- **Ages 3-5**: ≤1 hour/day (WHO/AAP) -- educational content preferred
- **Ages 6-12**: no fixed limit (AAP 2026), but 3+ hours/day associated with elevated cardiometabolic risk; 7+ hours/day linked to cortical thinning
- **Ages 13-18**: no fixed limit, but 3-6 hours/day common and associated with measurable metabolic risk accumulation
- **Risk multiplier**: sleep disruption amplifies the impact of screen time at all ages; short sleepers show markedly higher cardiometabolic risk at the same screen exposure levels
- **Content matters**: passive scrolling carries different risks than interactive or educational use
