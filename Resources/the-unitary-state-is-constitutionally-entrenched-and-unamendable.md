## Summary

Indonesia's unitary form is not just a political choice — since the 2002 constitutional reform it is legally locked in. Article 1(1) of the 1945 Constitution states that Indonesia is a unitary state in the form of a republic, and the fourth amendment added Article 37(5): “Particularly, regarding the form of the unitary state of the Republic of Indonesia, there can be no change” [@tobing2015]. Even the amendment process itself cannot convert Indonesia into a federation.

During the 1999–2002 amendment process, proposals to turn Indonesia into a federal state were floated by small groups and discussed, but they “didn't gain enough support and was not accepted” [@tobing2015]. The amendments that did pass — which radically democratised the system — preserved Article 1(1) intact while adding wide-ranging regional autonomy provisions (amended Articles 18, 18A, and 18B, including recognition of "special and distinct" regions) [@bertrand2019; @encyclopaediabritannica2026]. The implicit settlement: democratise and decentralise, but never federalise.

The entrenchment of unity reflects the founding ideology that the nation — imagined as one community forged by anti-colonial struggle — cannot be divided into constituent republics. Jakob Tobing, chairman of the parliamentary commission that drafted the 2002 amendments, states plainly that the unitary state has been supported by Indonesians as essential to Indonesia's existence [@tobing2015].

## Key Points

- Art. 1(1) of the 1945 Constitution: Indonesia is a unitary state in the form of a republic.
- Art. 37(5), added in 2002, makes the unitary form of the state unamendable.
- Federal proposals during 1999–2002 gained no support and were rejected.
- Autonomy and decentralisation were constitutionalised instead (Arts. 18, 18A, 18B).
