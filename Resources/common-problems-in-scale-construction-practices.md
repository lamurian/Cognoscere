## Summary

Common practices in psychological scale construction often prioritize reliability over validity, leading to instruments with poor content coverage. @steger2022 demonstrate that item sampling procedures emphasizing internal consistency (e.g., selecting items with highest factor loadings or item-total correlations) can artificially narrow the content of a scale, a phenomenon known as the attenuation paradox (@loevinger1954).

@flake2017 found that validity evidence of both existing and author-developed scales was commonly lacking, with coefficient alpha often being the only psychometric evidence reported. This overreliance on reliability as the primary quality indicator means that many published scales may have adequate internal consistency but poor construct coverage and content validity.

The problem is compounded by the fact that content validity is often just asserted rather than empirically demonstrated. Content validity is mostly qualitative in nature, based on construct definitions and expert judgments, making it especially hard to evaluate for constructs that lack clear definitions. Researchers may select highly homogeneous item sets because such items produce impressive reliability coefficients, but this homogeneity means the scale only partially reflects the target construct.

## Key Points

- Common item sampling emphasizes reliability over validity (@steger2022)
- Coefficient alpha is often the only psychometric evidence reported (@flake2017)
- The attenuation paradox: maximizing reliability can narrow construct coverage (@loevinger1954)
- Content validity is often asserted rather than empirically demonstrated
- Homogeneous item sets produce high reliability but may only partially measure the construct
