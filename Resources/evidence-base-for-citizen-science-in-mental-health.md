## Summary

The first systematic review of citizen science in mental health identified only nine eligible studies across Australia, Belgium, Canada, Denmark, Netherlands, Spain, the UK and the USA — all high-income countries — spanning eating disorders, serious mental illness, autism spectrum disorder, addiction, bipolar disorder, and indigenous youth mental health [@todowede2023]. Study designs included quantitative, qualitative, systematic review, RCT, mixed-methods, and citizen science methodology papers. This thin evidence base indicates the field is in its infancy, yet feasibility is established [@todowede2023].

Documented benefits for citizen scientists are substantial: empowerment, feeling better informed about their condition, improved mental health and wellbeing, acceptance of illness, increased help-seeking behaviour, reduced stress and stigma, enhanced coping skills, improved self-esteem, and social connectivity [@todowede2023]. Researchers benefit from inclusive research approaches, efficient task completion by trained crowds (e.g., annotating behavioural features for autism detection), and the ability to recruit and retain people with serious mental illness online at scale [@todowede2023].

The main documented harm is burden: the considerable time and energy commitment required. High dropout rates were reported, driven by busy schedules, technical difficulties, significant life events, preference for face-to-face interaction, and lack of acknowledgement [@todowede2023]. Data quality depended on diverse participation, project-specific training, and strategies to prevent data contamination between participant groups (e.g., separating chat rooms) [@todowede2023].

## Key Points

- **Feasibility established**: Nine studies confirm citizen science works in mental health — for data collection, analysis, and intervention delivery — but all evidence is from high-income countries [@todowede2023].
- **Citizen benefits**: empowerment, improved mental health, wellbeing, quality of life, recovery, social function, help-seeking, reduced stigma; youth gained resilience against substance abuse, depression and anxiety [@todowede2023].
- **Motivation to participate**: forming social relationships, connectivity, belonging to a group or community [@todowede2023].
- **Researcher benefits**: reaching people with serious mental illness online (crowdsourcing confirmed feasible and acceptable for diagnosis confirmation, self-report, outcome collation, intervention delivery); efficient, accurate crowd annotation [@todowede2023].
- **Harm and burden**: time and energy commitment can be burdensome; hostile messages posted by participants caused distress (documented in two of seven studies in one systematic review) [@todowede2023].
- **Dropout drivers**: lack of time, platform technical issues, life events, preference for face-to-face, lack of acknowledgement [@todowede2023].
- **Engagement success factors**: feeling endorsed and supported, community belonging, acknowledgement of contributions, IT support, and professional facilitators [@todowede2023].
- **Data quality mechanisms**: diversity of contributors, project-specific training, separating participant groups to prevent data contamination, moderation [@todowede2023].
- **Context gap**: sparse use in low- and middle-income countries attributed to unfamiliarity with the approach, cultural differences around online research, and digital exclusion; decolonising digital citizen science is a stated research priority [@todowede2023].
