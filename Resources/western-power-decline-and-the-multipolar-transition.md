## Summary

The post-1945 Western-led international order faces its most significant structural challenge since the Cold War. The shift from unipolarity to multipolarity is driven by economic redistribution, institutional fragmentation, and the assertive foreign policies of revisionist powers. Between 1750 and 1900, the West's share of global industrial production rose from less than a quarter to over four-fifths. By 2023, emerging and developing economies accounted for 58.9% of global output, up 7.6 percentage points since 2008 — the year developing economies first surpassed advanced nations in aggregate GDP (@grgi2025).

The Munich Security Report 2025 characterizes this as "multipolarization," noting that while many Global South actors see opportunity in a more inclusive order, the dominant effect has been growing polarization between competing visions of international governance. The report calls for "depolarization" through institutional reform, but concedes that competing order models are blocking joint responses to shared crises (@bunde2025).

The U.S. remains the world's largest economy and possesses unmatched military reach, alliance networks, and technological capacity. However, domestic political polarization, fiscal strain, and policy inconsistency — particularly under the Trump administration's tariff wars and withdrawal from multilateral frameworks — have eroded alliance confidence. European allies are accelerating defense autonomy discussions, while Global South nations increasingly assert independent foreign policies rather than aligning with either bloc.

Critically, Western decline is not absolute decline but relative decline. The West retains structural power in finance, technology standards, and military alliances. The Oxford IPE review argues that narratives of Western economic decline disconnect from evidence of continued structural influence, suggesting the transition is more gradual and contested than popular accounts imply. The question is whether existing institutions can adapt to accommodate rising powers before systemic stress produces conflict.

## Key Points

- Emerging economies hold 58.9% of global GDP (2023 IMF data), up from surpassing advanced economies first in 2008
- Munich Security Report 2025 frames this as "multipolarization" with negative effects currently prevailing
- U.S. retains structural advantages but domestic divisions and policy inconsistency erode alliance confidence
- Western decline is relative, not absolute — structural power in finance, tech, and alliances persists
- Institutional reform needed to prevent systemic stress from producing conflict
