## Summary

State resilience refers to the situation-specific, fluctuating capacity of an individual to maintain or restore adaptive functioning during and after exposure to adversity. Unlike trait resilience, which is conceptualised as a relatively stable personality characteristic, state resilience varies across time, situations, and developmental stages — it is activated, mobilised, and deployed in response to specific stressors rather than existing as a fixed attribute [@helmreich2017; @farchi2025].

The distinction between trait and state resilience parallels the well-established trait-state distinction in personality psychology (e.g., state vs trait anxiety). Luthans et al. (2007) position resilience as a state-like component within the Psychological Capital (PsyCap) construct, alongside efficacy, hope, and optimism. In this framework, resilience is "state-like" — open to development and change, influenced by both individual and environmental factors, and fluctuating in response to situational demands rather than being heritable and fixed over time like a trait [@luthans2007]. Helmreich et al. (2017) note that a sensitivity analysis in their Cochrane protocol distinguishes between scales measuring resilience as a personality characteristic (trait) versus resilience as an outcome of intervention (state-like), acknowledging that the underlying construct differs [@helmreich2017].

State resilience manifests as the dynamic deployment of psychological resources when adversity strikes. Farchi and Peled-Avram (2025) describe this through the ART framework: individuals acknowledge their available coping resources, reframe threats as challenges, and tailor their response by matching resources to situational demands. This three-step process is inherently state-like — it operates in real time, varies across situations, and depends on the interaction between the individual's resource pool and the specific characteristics of the stressor. The state character of resilience means that the same individual may exhibit high resilience in one context and low resilience in another, depending on which resources are accessible, how the situation is appraised, and what coping strategies are available in that moment [@farchi2025].

Stainton et al. (2018) further conceptualise resilience as a multimodal dynamic process that integrates cognitive, emotional, social, and physiological subsystems — none of which are static. At the neurobiological level, prefrontal-amygdala circuitry enables cognitive control over emotional reactivity, HPA axis flexibility provides physiological substrate, and heart rate variability reflects autonomic regulation capacity. These systems fluctuate moment-to-moment, supporting the characterisation of resilience as state-like rather than trait-like at the mechanistic level [@stainton2018; @kalisch2014]. The umbrella review by Abate et al. (2024) provides pooled effect sizes showing that resilience-promoting interventions (d = 0.42) and adversity-protective factors (d = 0.31) significantly increase resilience, confirming that resilience is malleable and responsive to intervention — a property inconsistent with a purely trait-based conceptualisation [@abate2024].

## Key Points

- State resilience = situation-specific, fluctuating capacity activated during adversity
- Luthans et al. position resilience as state-like in PsyCap: open to development, not heritable/fixed
- ART framework: state resilience emerges through acknowledgement, reframing, and tailoring in real time
- Same individual can exhibit high resilience in one context and low in another (state fluctuation)
- Neurobiological substrates (PFC-amygdala, HPA axis, HRV) fluctuate moment-to-moment, supporting state character
- Interventions significantly increase resilience (d = 0.42), confirming malleability
