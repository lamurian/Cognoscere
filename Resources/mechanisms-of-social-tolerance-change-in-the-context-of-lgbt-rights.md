## Summary

The mechanisms driving changes in social tolerance toward LGBT people operate through multiple pathways that have been quantitatively documented. First, the contact hypothesis holds that increased visibility and interpersonal contact with openly LGBT individuals reduces prejudice. Democratic freedoms of expression and association enable this visibility, as LGBT people can live more openly and participate in public life [@flores2023].

Second, generational replacement is a key driver. Younger cohorts consistently show higher acceptance of LGBTI people than older cohorts across countries, leading to gradual societal attitude shifts as older generations are replaced. This pattern is documented across multiple waves of the World Values Survey and European Social Survey.

Third, legal changes create feedback effects on social attitudes. Anti-discrimination laws and relationship recognition signal social norms, which in turn shift public opinion. The Williams Institute studies find that legal rights and social acceptance reinforce each other -- countries with both legal protections and high public acceptance show the strongest economic performance [@badgett2018].

Fourth, economic development itself drives tolerance through what Inglehart's post-materialist theory describes: as societies become wealthier and basic needs are met, populations place greater value on individual autonomy, self-expression, and minority rights [@badgett2019]. This creates a potential virtuous cycle where economic growth enables greater tolerance, which in turn enables fuller economic participation by previously excluded groups, further driving growth.

However, these mechanisms are not universal. In contexts where democratic institutions are weak or backsliding, attempts to increase LGBT acceptance can provoke backlash. Anti-LGBT rhetoric may be weaponized as a populist political strategy, exploiting identity-based divisions to consolidate power. The Flores et al. (2023) study documents this dynamic in Indonesia, Brazil, and Poland, where democratic backsliding co-occurred with intensified anti-LGBT mobilization.

## Key Points

- Contact hypothesis: visibility and interpersonal contact reduce prejudice
- Generational replacement: younger cohorts consistently more accepting
- Legal feedback loops: anti-discrimination laws shift social norms and attitudes
- Post-materialist values: economic development shifts priorities toward autonomy and minority rights
- Backlash risk: in weakening democracies, tolerance efforts can provoke counter-mobilization
- Transnational anti-gender movements: organized opposition funded by conservative networks
