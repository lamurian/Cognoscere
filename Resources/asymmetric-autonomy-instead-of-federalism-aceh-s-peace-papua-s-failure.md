## Summary

Where separatism was strongest, Indonesia chose asymmetric autonomy rather than federation. After democratisation in 1998, two special autonomy laws were passed in 2001 — one for Aceh (Law 18/2001) and one for Papua (Law 21/2001, OTSUS). The outcomes diverged sharply. Aceh's 2001 law failed to stop the GAM insurgency because it was imposed rather than negotiated; it was only after the 2004 tsunami and the Helsinki mediation that the far more detailed Law 11/2006 (LOGA) was negotiated, granting Aceh authority over all sectors except foreign policy, defence, security, monetary and fiscal policy, and justice — plus 70% of oil and gas revenues and the unprecedented right to local political parties [@bertrand2019]. The Free Aceh Movement disarmed, became Partai Aceh, and won the 2006 and 2012 gubernatorial elections; conflict ended [@bertrand2019].

Papua is the counter-case. The 2001 OTSUS law was also imposed, left out Papuan demands (a referendum, revisiting the 1969 Act of Free Choice), was undermined by the 2003 division of the province into Papua and West Papua, and was never properly implemented; the Papuan People's Assembly was weakened, fiscal transfers fueled corruption rather than development, and poverty stayed around 37%. Low-level conflict continues, and Papuan groups still reject OTSUS [@bertrand2019].

Bertrand's verdict: after the constitutional transition, Indonesia "today resembles more a highly asymmetrical form of federalism than the highly unitary and centralised state" of earlier decades. But crucially, this quasi-federal outcome was achieved through negotiated autonomy and district-level decentralisation while keeping the constitutionally entrenched unitary form — evidence that Indonesia's system accommodates diversity by avoiding formal federalism, whose every feature remains tainted by the RIS experience [@bertrand2019].

## Key Points

- Aceh: negotiated Law 11/2006 (Helsinki process) ended the separatist war; local parties and fiscal generosity were key.
- Papua: imposed, diluted, and poorly implemented 2001 OTSUS failed; the province was even split in two.
- Negotiation and credible commitment — not the form of state — explain Aceh's success.
- Indonesia now functions as an asymmetrical, quasi-federal state within a formally unitary constitution.
